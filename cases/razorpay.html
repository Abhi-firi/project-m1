How I Would Build a Predictable Sales Pipeline and Forecasting Engine for Razorpay

Case study 4 of 6 in my "Proof of Thinking" series — demonstrating how I reason through growth problems using real frameworks, disclosed assumptions and no fabricated data.

Important Disclaimer

This is an independent hypothetical business case based on publicly available information about Razorpay and general B2B sales and forecasting principles. It is not affiliated with Razorpay and does not use its internal sales, pipeline or merchant data.

All targets, funnel volumes, conversion rates, forecast figures and merchant-volume scenarios in this case study are hypothetical planning assumptions. They are not Razorpay's actual targets, internal data or forecasts.

01Executive Summary

A sales team can generate hundreds of leads and still struggle to predict how much business it will close. The underlying problem may not be lead volume alone — it could be poor prospect selection, weak qualification, low conversion between funnel stages, slow follow-ups or forecasts based on optimistic assumptions rather than actual buyer signals.

In this hypothetical case, I explore how Razorpay could build a more predictable acquisition engine for a selected segment of mid-market Indian businesses.

My approach connects five activities into one operating system:

  1. Identify merchants with strong product fit and meaningful payment-processing potential.
  2. Prioritize accounts based on evidence rather than lead volume alone.
  3. Track conversion and drop-offs across the sales funnel.
  4. Forecast deals using stage evidence, expected close dates and merchant potential.
  5. Use weekly performance data to improve sales productivity.

The proposed initial 90-day pilot targets eight newly activated merchants, with an illustrative funnel of 1,000 researched target accounts. These are planning assumptions for this case study, not Razorpay's actual targets, internal data or forecast.

The goal is not simply to generate more leads. It is to create a sales process where the team can explain where growth will come from, what could prevent it and which actions are most likely to improve the outcome.

02Understanding the Business Problem

Imagine Razorpay wants to improve new-merchant acquisition in a selected mid-market segment. The sales team has access to a large potential customer base, but management wants a clearer view of which prospects are most likely to activate, how long conversion takes and how much business the pipeline can reasonably deliver.

For this case, I assume the following challenges need to be investigated:

  • Sales effort is spread across businesses with very different transaction potential.
  • Some leads enter the pipeline without a clearly established business need.
  • Deals move through stages without consistent exit criteria.
  • Follow-ups and technical or commercial discussions sometimes delay decisions.
  • Forecasts may depend too heavily on salespeople's subjective confidence.
  • A signed agreement does not necessarily mean the merchant will immediately generate meaningful payment volume.

These are hypotheses to validate, not claims about Razorpay's actual sales operations.

The core business question

How can Razorpay build a repeatable sales pipeline that converts the right merchants into active customers and makes new-business forecasting more reliable?

What success should look like

The system should help management answer four questions at any point: Are we targeting the right businesses? Is our pipeline large and qualified enough to support the target? Which funnel stage is limiting conversions? How much of the expected business is supported by real evidence?

03Define the Ideal Customer Profile (ICP)

My first decision would be to avoid targeting every business that accepts digital payments. Instead, I would begin with a defined segment of Indian mid-market businesses that already have digital transactions or a clear near-term need to accept them.

Initial segments to investigate

A. D2C and e-commerce businesses — smooth checkout experiences, multiple payment methods, better visibility into payment performance.

B. Education businesses — collecting course fees, handling recurring payments, simplifying online fee collection.

C. SaaS businesses — recurring billing, subscription payments, accepting payments from international customers where supported.

These use cases align with the kinds of solutions Razorpay publicly offers. Choosing the best segment requires validation using actual conversion, merchant-volume and sales-cycle data.

ICP scoring model

FactorWeightWhat I would evaluate
Payment potential30%Expected transaction volume and growth
Product fit25%Match with relevant Razorpay capabilities
Pain intensity20%Existing payment, checkout or collection problems
Buying readiness15%Clear need, decision-maker access and implementation timing
Sales feasibility10%Likely effort, integration complexity and time to activate
Weighted score = Σ (factor score ÷ 5 × factor weight) — out of 100

Illustrative prioritization

  • Tier 1 — 80–100: Prioritize for personalized outreach and early discovery.
  • Tier 2 — 60–79: Run targeted outreach and qualify further.
  • Tier 3 — Below 60: Use lower-cost nurture or revisit when stronger buying signals appear.

These thresholds are proposed starting points, not validated benchmarks.

My decision: Test D2C/e-commerce and SaaS prospects as separate cohorts rather than committing all resources to one. After the first few weeks, compare qualified-opportunity rates, expected payment potential, activation rates and sales effort before allocating more capacity.

04Build a Prioritized Target-Account List

A strong ICP is useful only when it translates into a practical prospecting plan. For the first 90 days, I would build a list of 1,000 researched target accounts across the selected segments — a proposed pilot input, not a claim about market size.

Information to collect

Company name and website, business model and industry, approximate business scale where credible, existing checkout/payment experience, likely payment use case, decision-maker contact details, a specific reason for outreach, ICP score and priority tier, and outreach status/next action/owner.

Account prioritization signals

Higher priority to businesses showing rapid expansion into new products or geographies, launch of a new online sales channel, a subscription/recurring-payment model, a checkout experience that could benefit from improvement, increasing transaction complexity, or a relevant business initiative indicating active need.

I would distinguish between verified facts and sales hypotheses. A new job posting might indicate investment in a function, but would not prove a payment problem exists.

Operating rule: Every Tier 1 account must have a reason for being prioritized. A large company name by itself is not sufficient.

05Design the Pipeline Generation Strategy

I would use three complementary channels rather than relying on a single source of leads.

Channel 1: Targeted outbound

Use account research to contact relevant decision-makers with a message connected to their business model. For e-commerce, explore checkout friction and payment reliability; for SaaS, focus on recurring billing or international payments. The objective of the first conversation is to establish whether a meaningful business problem exists, not to force a product pitch.

Channel 2: Inbound lead qualification

Prioritize using business scale and likely payment volume, stated use case, readiness to integrate, expected implementation timing and product fit. A high number of inbound leads is not automatically a high-quality pipeline — qualification must happen early.

Channel 3: Partnerships and ecosystem referrals

Investigate referral opportunities through e-commerce agencies, implementation partners, SaaS ecosystems and relevant business networks — reaching merchants when they are already making decisions about their digital infrastructure.

07Calculate the Funnel Backwards from the Target

I would start with the desired business outcome and calculate the activity required to support it. For this case, the proposed 90-day pilot target is eight newly activated merchants. The following funnel is illustrative — a working hypothesis to test, not an established benchmark.

The funnel mathematically supports approximately nine wins, providing some headroom against the eight-merchant pilot target. Actual results would depend on sales-cycle length, account quality, team capacity and activation delays.

Why work backwards?

If the target is missed, the funnel helps identify the issue: low engagement points to weak account selection or messaging; meetings without qualification point to targeting or discovery issues; proposals without closes point to value justification, pricing or competition; closed deals without activation point to onboarding problems. The solution should match the bottleneck — increasing outreach volume is not always the right response.

The table counts accounts at each stage, not necessarily unique people contacted. I would also report stage conversion by cohort and acquisition channel so strong performance in one segment doesn't conceal weak performance in another.

08Build a More Reliable Sales Forecast

A pipeline is useful for forecasting only when opportunity data is current and stage progression reflects evidence. I would maintain three complementary views.

  • A. Commit forecast — strong evidence of closing within the period: buyer confirmation, completed decision steps, commercial alignment, a credible close date. A salesperson's confidence alone is insufficient.
  • B. Best-case forecast — could close within the period but still has meaningful unresolved risks (pending technical validation, budget approval, stakeholder alignment).
  • C. Pipeline forecast — all qualified opportunities expected to close within the period, including deals not yet mature for commit.

Weighted forecast formula

Weighted expected revenue = Σ (deal's expected revenue × probability of closing within the forecast period)

The key phrase is within the forecast period. A deal that may close eventually should not be counted as though it will certainly close this month.

Illustrative example

OpportunityExpected revenue (assumed)Close probability
Deal A₹10 lakh80%
Deal B₹8 lakh50%
Deal C₹5 lakh20%

Deal A: ₹10L × 80% = ₹8L · Deal B: ₹8L × 50% = ₹4L · Deal C: ₹5L × 20% = ₹1L

Total weighted expected revenue = ₹13 lakh

These values are purely illustrative and are not Razorpay deal values or pricing estimates. The example shows the method, not a real company forecast.

How I would improve forecast quality

I would review stage age (has the opportunity stalled?), close-date movement (has it slipped repeatedly?), buyer engagement, unresolved blockers (security, integration, legal, commercial), whether a specific next step is agreed, and historical calibration of stage probabilities against actual outcomes. Until sufficient data exists, probabilities should be labelled as provisional assumptions.

09Connect Merchant Acquisition to Payment Potential

For a payments business, the number of signed customers alone does not tell the complete story. Two merchants may both be activated but generate very different transaction volumes.

Illustrative merchant-volume model

Assume the pilot activates eight merchants, each eventually processing an average of ₹25 lakh per month:

8 merchants × ₹25 lakh = ₹2 crore in monthly payment volume

This is a planning scenario, not a guaranteed result. Actual volumes may be lower or higher, and merchants may ramp at different speeds.

Track three distinct outcomes

  1. Acquisition outcome — how many qualified merchants have been won?
  2. Activation outcome — how many have completed onboarding and started processing?
  3. Business outcome — how much actual payment volume, compared with the expected use case?

I would monitor both expected and actual volumes at 30, 60 and 90 days after activation.

Merchant payment volume is not the same as Razorpay's recognized revenue — actual economics depend on commercial terms, payment methods, pricing and costs. Any financial model for a real decision should distinguish gross payment volume from revenue and contribution.

My decision framework

At the end of the pilot, three questions decide what happens next: Scale — are opportunities converting, merchants activating, and unit economics showing promise? Optimize — is there clear demand, but one stage or constraint limiting results? Reassess — are the segments, targeting or value proposition failing to produce enough qualified demand despite disciplined execution? The answer should be based on observed results, not on whether the team completed its planned activities.

The central principle:

Build a pipeline the business can explain, a forecast it can defend, and an acquisition process it can improve continuously.

I would recommend a focused 90-day pilot before expanding the acquisition model across additional merchant segments. The most important output would not be a dashboard alone — it would be a repeatable decision-making system that connects prospect selection, sales execution, funnel performance, forecasting and merchant outcomes.
